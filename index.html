Understanding Digital Forensics

Before examining individual cases, this section establishes the discipline, its purpose, its evidence sources and the principles that make an investigation reliable.

CORE DEFINITION

What is Digital Forensics?

Digital forensics is the disciplined process of identifying, preserving, acquiring, examining, analysing and reporting digital data in a way that maintains its integrity and allows findings to be independently reviewed.

It is used to reconstruct events involving computers, phones, networks, cloud services, storage devices, applications and other digital systems. It is not simply “recovering deleted files”; it is a method for answering investigative questions using traceable evidence.

A forensic conclusion must be supported by evidence, documented methods and stated limitations—not assumptions.

Where can digital evidence come from?

Digital evidence may be volatile or persistent, local or remote, structured or unstructured.

▣

Computer & Disk

Files, file-system metadata, deleted data, browser history, registry artifacts, partitions and boot records.

▤

Memory

Running processes, active network connections, loaded modules, encryption material and other volatile artifacts.

⌁

Network

Packet captures, DNS queries, firewall events, proxy logs, authentication events and traffic metadata.

▧

Mobile

Calls, messages, app data, location artifacts, media, device backups and usage traces.

☁

Cloud & SaaS

Identity logs, cloud audit trails, object access, email events, API activity and configuration history.

▶

Images & Video

EXIF metadata, encoding information, frames, timestamps, edits, provenance and visual inconsistencies.

The forensic investigation workflow

A practical end-to-end workflow. NIST SP 800-86 describes four core forensic phases—collection, examination, analysis and reporting—while identification and preservation are essential preparation and evidence-handling activities.

Chain of Custody

A chronological record of who collected, handled, transferred, accessed and stored an item of evidence, including dates, times, purpose and signatures/identifiers. It supports accountability and helps demonstrate that evidence remained controlled.

Integrity & Hashing

A cryptographic hash such as SHA-256 produces a digest for a file or forensic image. Matching hashes before and after a transfer support the conclusion that the copied data is unchanged. A hash does not prove who created a file or whether the original content was truthful.

03 / INVESTIGATION QUALITY

Research & evidence standards

ASource hierarchy

Prefer official standards, vendor documentation, government statements, original datasets and direct employer listings. Use secondary reporting to add context, not to replace primary evidence.

BSeparate fact from inference

Label confirmed facts, attributed claims, technical hypotheses and unknowns. Do not convert a plausible attack path into a claimed forensic finding.

CReproducibility

Record acquisition details, hashes, tool names and versions, commands/settings, timestamps, time zones and limitations so another examiner can review the work.

DEthical & lawful handling

Use authorized evidence and controlled lab images. Minimize personal data exposure, respect privacy and preserve originals.
